# Novel outline

> This document defines the architecture of the book as a whole.
>
> The intended form is a **linked-story / mosaic novel**: a collection of substantial short stories or novella-length episodes set in one shared fantasy world. Each story should stand on its own, while also contributing to a larger network of characters, consequences, places, rumours, objects, institutions, and unresolved events.
>
> The book should feel as though the reader is moving through a real society rather than following a party of characters who conveniently know one another.

---

## Core concept

The book takes place inside one canonical fantasy world.

Different stories may:

- focus on completely different protagonists;
- occur in different regions, social classes, professions, cultures, or communities;
- use different supporting casts;
- overlap in chronology;
- revisit the same event from another person's perspective;
- feature characters who disappear for several stories and later return;
- introduce temporary characters who exist because that particular story needs them;
- reveal indirect connections between people who never meet.

The world is the constant.

The cast is fluid.

The reader gradually discovers that apparently separate lives are connected through a loose network similar to real-world "six degrees of separation".

Connections should usually feel **incidental, social, economic, geographical, political, professional, familial, historical, or magical** rather than artificially engineered.

A connection does **not** require two characters to meet.

Examples:

- a healer treats Character A and later becomes Character D's neighbour;
- Character B works for a merchant whose missing shipment caused the shortage affecting Character F;
- Character C hears a distorted tavern story about an event the reader witnessed accurately in Story 1;
- Character E owns an object previously carried by a minor character in Story 2;
- two protagonists independently know the same priest, ferryman, archivist, criminal, landlord, or government clerk;
- a law passed because of one story quietly changes the options available to people three stories later.

---

## Intended reading experience

The reader should experience three levels of story simultaneously.

### 1. Immediate story

Each individual story has:

- a protagonist or small focal cast;
- a concrete desire, problem, threat, mystery, relationship, obligation, or opportunity;
- meaningful escalation;
- consequences;
- an emotionally and narratively satisfying ending.

A reader should not need to remember twenty names from previous stories simply to understand the current one.

### 2. Human network

Across stories, the reader begins recognising:

- names;
- families;
- workplaces;
- neighbourhoods;
- institutions;
- guilds;
- rumours;
- objects;
- political decisions;
- historical events;
- shared acquaintances.

These recognitions provide reward without turning the book into a continuity exam.

### 3. World arc

The world should subtly change over the course of the book.

This may include:

- a political transition;
- social unrest;
- a magical phenomenon;
- a war approaching or ending;
- a new trade route;
- disease or recovery;
- religious change;
- the decline of an institution;
- technological or magical innovation;
- a disaster and its aftermath;
- a mystery whose pieces appear in unrelated lives.

The world arc does not have to become an "everyone joins together for the final battle" plot.

Its purpose is to make every story belong to the same historical moment.

---

## Structural model

The default structure should be approximately **6-10 substantial stories**.

A useful target for the first book is:

- 7-9 stories;
- approximately 7,000-15,000 words per story;
- some stories shorter or longer where justified;
- approximately 75,000-110,000 words total.

These are planning ranges, not rigid limits.

Every story should be long enough to establish:

- a specific slice of life;
- the protagonist's normal state;
- a disturbance;
- character interaction;
- at least one meaningful decision;
- consequence;
- a satisfying local resolution.

Avoid writing stories that feel like fragments included only to introduce a character for later.

---

## Character participation model

Characters should be classified by **narrative function**, not by importance as people.

### Anchor characters

Characters whose lives span much of the book.

They may:

- lead multiple stories;
- appear prominently in other characters' stories;
- undergo a long-running personal arc;
- act as recognisable points of continuity.

The book does not require a single central protagonist.

Recommended first-book count: 2-4.

### Recurring characters

Characters who appear meaningfully in several stories but do not dominate the book.

They may:

- be a friend, relative, rival, colleague, client, neighbour, official, healer, innkeeper, priest, criminal, scholar, or employer;
- have their own life that continues off-page;
- become a future protagonist.

Recommended count: as needed, but keep their roles distinct.

### Bridge characters

Characters whose main structural function is to connect otherwise separate social groups.

A bridge character can be minor in page count but important to the network.

Examples:

- a courier;
- magistrate;
- physician;
- travelling merchant;
- archivist;
- sex worker;
- priest;
- ferry operator;
- teacher;
- guild clerk;
- bounty hunter;
- landlord.

Use sparingly. A bridge character should still feel like a person, not a plot cable.

### Story-local characters

Characters created for one particular story.

They can be:

- lovers;
- antagonists;
- customers;
- victims;
- witnesses;
- family members;
- travelling companions;
- officials;
- residents;
- guards;
- servants;
- strangers.

They do not need to reappear.

However, consequences involving them may persist.

### Background continuity characters

Small recurring figures who make the world feel inhabited.

Examples:

- the same exhausted customs officer;
- a baker whose shop appears in multiple neighbourhood stories;
- a guard captain mentioned more often than seen;
- a famous singer whose songs are heard across the book;
- a newspaper editor whose headlines shape public opinion.

These should remain light touches.

## The six-degrees rule

The character network should resemble real social life.

### Rule 1 — Not everyone knows everyone

Do not create a single friendship group and merely rotate POV.

Characters should inhabit different circles.

### Rule 2 — Connections may be indirect

Character A may know B.

B may have previously worked for C.

C may be the estranged sibling of D.

D may employ E.

E may never know A exists.

That is sufficient.

### Rule 3 — Connections can be asymmetrical

One character may know another by reputation without the second character knowing them.

### Rule 4 — Connections can be wrong

Rumour, prejudice, memory, propaganda, and incomplete information may distort how one character understands another.

### Rule 5 — Avoid coincidence overload

If every stranger turns out to be someone's sibling, former lover, or secret heir, the world becomes small.

Prefer ordinary connective tissue:

- work;
- neighbourhoods;
- commerce;
- public institutions;
- travel;
- religion;
- government;
- magic systems;
- shared history.

### Rule 6 — Let some connections remain invisible

The reader does not need every relationship explained.

A background name can simply recur.

## Main arc

The book should contain two kinds of arc.

### Personal arcs

Several long-running characters develop across multiple stories.

Possible arc shapes:

- ambition -> success -> cost -> reassessment;
- isolation -> reluctant connection -> trust -> betrayal or belonging;
- certainty -> contradiction -> doubt -> new worldview;
- survival -> stability -> responsibility for others;
- resentment -> understanding without reconciliation;
- dependence -> independence -> chosen interdependence.

### World arc

A larger change moves through the background of the book.

It should appear differently depending on who is affected.

For example, the same decree might be:

- a bureaucratic nuisance to a merchant;
- life-saving protection to a minority group;
- lost income to a guard;
- political ammunition to an aristocrat;
- irrelevant gossip to someone living far away;
- the cause of a later migration.

The purpose is not to tell the reader what everyone thinks.

The purpose is to demonstrate that world events have uneven consequences.

## Story-level requirements

Each story should define:

- **Primary POV**
- **Secondary important characters**
- **Location**
- **Time position**
- **Local premise**
- **Starting state**
- **Inciting disturbance**
- **What the protagonist wants**
- **What the protagonist actually needs, if different**
- **Primary opposition**
- **Key decision**
- **Cost of that decision**
- **Local resolution**
- **Change carried into later stories**
- **Characters introduced**
- **Characters returning**
- **Connections to earlier stories**
- **Seeds planted for later stories**
- **Details that must be added to canon**

---

## Continuity layers

Track continuity at several levels.

### Hard continuity

Facts that must never drift:

- names;
- ages;
- family relationships;
- scars;
- species;
- magical abilities;
- deaths;
- marriages;
- geography;
- travel times;
- major historical dates.

### Soft continuity

Facts that may legitimately change:

- opinions;
- friendships;
- fashion;
- employment;
- wealth;
- injuries;
- habits;
- living arrangements;
- political loyalties.

When soft continuity changes, the change must be explainable.

### Perspective continuity

Different people can interpret the same event differently.

Example:

**Canonical fact:** a warehouse burned.

Possible perspectives:

- "rebels burned it";
- "the city guard burned it";
- "it was an accident";
- "the owner did it for insurance";
- "a summoned creature escaped".

These statements can coexist as character beliefs until canon establishes the truth.

---

## Repetition policy

When a previous event matters in a later story:

- give only the amount of context the current POV would naturally know;
- do not re-summarise the previous story;
- allow incorrect or incomplete recollection;
- avoid identical exposition across stories.

The reader should feel recognition, not repetition.

---

## Book opening

The first story should:

1. establish the tone of the world;
2. give the reader one deeply understandable life to enter;
3. demonstrate the fantasy setting through lived experience rather than a world-history lecture;
4. introduce at least one element capable of crossing into later stories;
5. resolve its local plot strongly enough that the reader understands the book's linked-story format can be trusted.

The first story does **not** need to introduce every important character.

It should deliberately leave most of the world unseen.

---

## Book ending

The final story should provide two forms of closure.

### Local closure

Its own protagonist's immediate story must resolve.

### Mosaic closure

The reader should be able to look backward and understand that the book captured one meaningful period in the world's life.

Useful final effects:

- an earlier minor action is revealed to have mattered;
- a recurring institution changes;
- a rumour becomes history;
- a long-running relationship reaches a new state;
- an object passes into new hands;
- a place seen repeatedly has changed;
- the consequences of the first story are visible from a completely different social position.

Avoid forcing every surviving character into the final scene.

The world should feel larger than the book.

## Example only — illustrative network

> The following is **not canon**. It demonstrates how the structure can work without making every character part of the same group.

### Story 1 — The apprentice

A young mapmaker falsifies a route permit so his sister can leave the capital before a district is quarantined.

He succeeds, but the forged route sends her caravan through a remote crossing.

### Story 2 — The innkeeper

Months later, an innkeeper in another town deals with an unexpected flood of travellers after authorities close the usual road.

The mapmaker never appears.

One traveller happens to be the sister from Story 1, but she is a supporting character and the innkeeper has no idea why her papers are unusual.

### Story 3 — The investigator

A government investigator is ordered to locate the source of several forged permits.

She interviews the mapmaker's former supervisor, not the mapmaker himself.

During the investigation she befriends a street physician.

### Story 4 — The physician

The physician becomes the protagonist of an unrelated story concerning illegal magical treatment.

The investigator appears briefly as a friend.

One of the physician's patients is the estranged father of the innkeeper from Story 2.

### Story 5 — The merchant

A merchant's business is collapsing because the quarantines have broken a trade route.

The merchant purchases an old map containing the apprentice's handwriting.

The merchant never learns who drew it.

### Story 6 — The sister

The sister from Story 1 becomes the POV character.

What looked like a successful escape in Story 1 is now revealed to have created an entirely different problem in her life.

### Why this works

The stories overlap without requiring:

- a team;
- a chosen-one plot;
- constant cameos;
- implausible family revelations;
- every character knowing one another.

The network emerges through ordinary life.

## Questions to answer before drafting Book 1

These should become explicit canon before prose generation begins.

### World

- What is the world called?
- What geographic area does Book 1 cover?
- What fantasy elements are normal parts of everyday life?
- What is rare, dangerous, illegal, sacred, or misunderstood?
- What institutions organise society?
- What creates class, privilege, exclusion, and mobility?
- How difficult is travel?
- How fast does information travel?
- What does ordinary work look like?

### Book-scale event

- What is changing during this book?
- Who notices first?
- Who benefits?
- Who is harmed?
- Who barely notices?
- What rumours exist about it?
- What facts are hidden from most characters?

### Character network

- Who are the 2-4 likely anchor characters?
- Which social circles do they inhabit?
- Where are the bridges between those circles?
- Which characters should never meet?
- Which connections should be known only to the reader?

### Ending

- What has definitely changed by the final story?
- Which personal arcs must close?
- Which threads deliberately remain open for later books?
- What image, place, object, relationship, or social change could give Book 1 a satisfying final echo?
