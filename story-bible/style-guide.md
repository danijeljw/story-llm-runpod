# Style guide

This file defines prose and narrative behaviour across the series.

It should prevent the writing from becoming generic, melodramatic, repetitive, or inconsistent when different stories use different POV characters.

---

## Voice

Use a **grounded, character-led fantasy voice**.

The world may contain extraordinary magic, creatures, cultures, institutions, or history, but characters should experience most of it as part of ordinary life.

Prefer:

- concrete observation;
- behaviour;
- dialogue;
- material consequences;
- specific sensory detail;
- social context;
- dry or situational humour where appropriate.

Avoid treating every fantasy detail as wondrous simply because it is fantastical to the reader.

If a character grew up around household spirits, they should not internally explain household spirits for the reader.

---

## POV

Default to **close third person**, one POV per scene.

A story should normally have one primary POV.

Multiple POVs are allowed where structurally justified, but do not switch casually inside a scene.

Narration may adopt some of the POV character's:

- vocabulary;
- prejudices;
- humour;
- level of education;
- attention patterns;
- professional knowledge.

The narrator is not omniscient unless a specific story explicitly requires it.

### Knowledge rule

The prose can only confidently state what the POV character:

- perceives;
- remembers;
- reasonably infers;
- already knows.

Objective canon may differ from what the character believes.

---

## Tense

Default to **past tense**.

Use another tense only for a deliberate formal reason.

Do not alternate tense accidentally.

---

## Prose

### General

Prefer clean, concrete prose over decorative prose.

Sentence length should vary with:

- character;
- scene tension;
- emotional state;
- subject matter.

Do not equate literary quality with long sentences.

### Description

Description should usually perform more than one job.

A useful description may reveal:

- place;
- class;
- occupation;
- mood;
- history;
- relationship;
- magical normality.

Weak:
> The tavern was old and cosy.

Stronger:
> Someone had polished the front tables for customers and left the rear ones tacky with last night's ale, which told Mara exactly how much the owner expected inspectors to notice.

### Worldbuilding

Worldbuilding should arrive through use.

Prefer:
> He pressed his travel seal against the warded gate and waited for the brass lettering to turn green.

Over:
> Travel seals had been invented two hundred years earlier and were used throughout the kingdom to regulate movement.

Historical explanation is appropriate only when the POV character has a reason to think about it.

---

## Dialogue

Dialogue should reveal:

- objective;
- status;
- familiarity;
- avoidance;
- history;
- misunderstanding.

Characters should not repeatedly tell one another facts they both know for the reader's benefit.

Allow:

- interruption;
- unfinished thoughts;
- deliberate topic changes;
- wrong assumptions;
- humour;
- silence.

Do not make every character witty.

---

## Character interiority

Use interiority to reveal how the POV character processes events.

Avoid continuously naming emotions.

Weak:
> She was angry and frustrated.

Prefer evidence:
> She rewrote the address twice because the first version had nearly torn through the paper.

Do not forbid emotion words entirely. Use them when they are the cleanest choice.

---

## Humour

Humour should emerge from:

- character;
- timing;
- social friction;
- understatement;
- absurd bureaucracy;
- incompatible expectations;
- practical reactions to fantasy circumstances.

Avoid forcing jokes into grief, danger, intimacy, or trauma simply to make the prose feel "clever".

---

## Romance and intimacy

Treat attraction as character-specific.

Avoid generic shorthand such as:

- "electric touch";
- "breath hitching" every time attraction occurs;
- everyone noticing the same physical features;
- instant emotional certainty;
- possessiveness automatically framed as romantic.

Romantic and sexual relationships should interact with:

- personality;
- history;
- boundaries;
- social conditions;
- power;
- practical life.

---

## Explicitness

Set per story and scene.

When sexual or violent material is present, its level of detail should serve:

- character;
- plot;
- tone;
- consequences.

Do not abruptly switch into a different prose genre for explicit scenes.

---

## Violence

Violence should have:

- physical consequences;
- social consequences;
- legal or political consequences where relevant;
- emotional consequences appropriate to the character.

Avoid consequence-free action unless the setting specifically supports it.

---

## Magic

Magic should remain subject to canonical rules.

Characters may:

- misunderstand magic;
- use slang for it;
- have different cultural explanations;
- disagree about ethics.

Narration should not silently change the system to solve a scene.

A magical solution should respect:

- cost;
- skill;
- access;
- legality;
- knowledge;
- established limits.

---

## Continuity

Every new scene inherits the exact state left by previous events.

Track:

- injuries;
- clothing when relevant;
- possessions;
- location;
- time;
- money;
- knowledge;
- promises;
- secrets;
- relationship changes.

Do not write as though characters have reset to their introduction state.

---

## Cross-story continuity

When characters recur:

- they have lived between appearances;
- they need not discuss the previous story;
- their priorities may have changed;
- they should retain consequences;
- they should not become exposition devices.

A reader may encounter someone first as:

- a protagonist;
- then a minor side character;
- then a name in a rumour;
- then not at all for half the book.

That is desirable.

---

## Exposition policy

Before explaining something, ask:

1. Does the POV character already know it?
2. Do they have a reason to think about it now?
3. Does the reader need it to understand the scene?
4. Can action demonstrate it instead?
5. Has it already been explained elsewhere?

If an answer makes the paragraph unnecessary, cut it.

---

## Emotional restraint

Do not underwrite emotion, but avoid inflating every feeling into a major emotional event.

Characters can:

- be annoyed without being furious;
- be attracted without falling in love;
- care about someone without articulating it;
- grieve without delivering a speech;
- forgive partially;
- remain conflicted;
- move on without full closure.

---

## Avoid

- repetitive physical beats;
- generic romance clichés;
- purple prose unless deliberately appropriate;
- characters explaining emotions they have already demonstrated;
- continuity resets between scenes;
- world-history lectures inserted into active scenes;
- every scene ending with a dramatic one-line revelation;
- excessive eyebrow raising, smirking, sighing, swallowing, breath catching, jaw clenching, and heart pounding;
- interchangeable dialogue voices;
- characters conveniently knowing information outside their experience;
- artificially linking every new character to the core cast;
- cameo appearances that serve no story purpose;
- treating rumours as objective truth;
- resolving complex social issues with one speech;
- making antagonists irrational merely so the protagonist can be correct;
- repeating full physical descriptions every time a recurring character appears;
- restating the previous chapter at the beginning of the next;
- every meaningful character secretly being nobility, magical royalty, a lost heir, or part of the same conspiracy unless deliberately required.

---

## Preferred recurring techniques

### Background consequences

Let major events appear in later stories through:

- price changes;
- queues;
- shortages;
- damaged buildings;
- newspaper notices;
- gossip;
- altered travel routes;
- policy;
- funerals;
- vacancies;
- new security;
- changed social behaviour.

### Perspective contrast

One story may frame an event as a victory.

Another may reveal its cost.

Neither POV needs to be the author's final verdict.

### Minor continuity

Small details make the world feel continuous:

- a shop changes its sign;
- someone still has an injury;
- a bridge remains under repair;
- a popular song spreads;
- a tavern has increased prices;
- a rumour becomes less accurate each time it travels.

---

## Scene quality check

Before accepting a scene, ask:

- Whose scene is it?
- What do they want right now?
- What changes before the scene ends?
- Is another character pursuing their own objective?
- Does the scene rely on unexplained coincidence?
- Is worldbuilding embedded naturally?
- Does dialogue sound specific to the speakers?
- Does the scene respect current relationship state?
- Is any paragraph explaining something already shown?
- Is there a meaningful reason this scene exists?
